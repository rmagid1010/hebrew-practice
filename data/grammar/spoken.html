How people actually talk עִבְרִית מְדוּבֶּרֶת

Textbook Hebrew and Tel Aviv Hebrew are the same language, but people take a lot of shortcuts in speech. Here are the ones you'll hear constantly. You'll understand people faster, and you'll sound less like a news anchor.

1. "My" is שֶׁלִּי, not a suffix

Textbooks teach possessive endings (בֵּיתִי = my house). In speech, people almost always use שֶׁל + ending after the noun:

SpokenFormal / writtenMeaning
הַבַּיִת שֶׁלִּיבֵּיתִיmy house
הַחֲבֵרִים שֶׁלְּךָחֲבֵרֶיךָyour friends
הָאוֹטוֹ שֶׁלָּהּ—her car

Note the הַ: it's "the house of mine". Exceptions you'll hear with suffixes: family and set phrases — אִשְׁתִּי (my wife), בַּעְלִי (my husband), אָחִי (my brother / bro).

2. Commands use the future tense

תָּבִיא (bring), תַּגִּיד (tell me), תִּשְׁמַע (listen), תְּסַמֵּס לִי (text me). The short textbook imperative sounds harsh or like a sign. "Don't" is always אַל + future: אַל תִּדְאַג.

"Let's…" = בּוֹא / בּוֹאִי / בּוֹאוּ + "we" future: בּוֹא נֵלֵךְ (let's go), בּוֹאוּ נַזְמִין (let's order). Or just יַאלְלָה + present plural: יַאלְלָה, הוֹלְכִים.

3. The present for the near future

Like English "I'm coming": אֲנִי מַגִּיעַ עוֹד עֶשֶׂר דַּקּוֹת = I'll be there in ten minutes. מָחָר אֲנִי עוֹבֵד = I'm working tomorrow.

4. Possession: יֵשׁ לִי / אֵין לִי

Hebrew has no verb "to have". You say "there is to me": יֵשׁ לִי אוֹטוֹ, אֵין לִי זְמַן. In speech people add אֶת before a definite thing — technically "wrong", universally said: יֵשׁ לִי אֶת הַמִּסְפָּר שֶׁלּוֹ = I have his number.

The same structure is behind בָּא לִי (I feel like), אִכְפַּת לִי (I care), נִמְאַס לִי (I'm fed up), קַר לִי (I'm cold), מַשְׁעֲמֵם לִי (I'm bored).

5. אֶת gets squashed

אֶת marks a definite direct object and you can't drop it — but in fast speech אֶת הַ… shrinks to "ta": תָּבִיא ת'מַּפְתְּחוֹת ("tavi tamaftechot") = bring the keys. You'll even see ת' written in texts.

6. Numbers: feminine, and the noun stays singular

Hebrew numbers have masculine and feminine forms, but in speech the feminine (counting) forms take over, and with money and some units the noun often stays singular:

  • עֶשְׂרִים שֶׁקֶל — 20 shekels (not שְׁקָלִים)
  • שָׁלוֹשׁ שֶׁקֶל — 3 shekels (textbook: שְׁלוֹשָׁה שְׁקָלִים)
  • חָמֵשׁ דַּקּוֹת — 5 minutes (this one is correct anyway: דַּקָּה is feminine)

7. אֵצֶל = at someone's place

Like French chez: נִפְגָּשִׁים אֶצְלִי? = Meet at my place? אֲנִי אֵצֶל דָּנִי = I'm at Dani's. אֶצְלְךָ הַכּוֹל בְּסֵדֶר? = All good with you?

8. Quoting with כָּזֶה / כָּזֹאת

Exactly like English "was like": וְהוּא כָּזֶה: "מָה?!" = and he was like, "what?!". Also a filler: הָיָה כָּזֶה מוּזָר = it was kind of weird.

10. Questions are just intonation

No word order change, no "do": אַתָּה בָּא. (You're coming.) → אַתָּה בָּא? (Are you coming?). Raise your voice at the end.

11. Dropping "I" in the past and future

The verb ending already tells you who: הָלַכְתִּי = I went, נְדַבֵּר = we'll talk. In the present, though, you need the pronoun: אֲנִי הוֹלֵךְ.

12. Groups of women get the masculine plural

Textbooks have אַתֶּן כְּתַבְתֶּן and הֵן תִּכְתּוֹבְנָה. In speech you'll mostly hear אַתֶּן כְּתַבְתֶּם and הֵן יִכְתְּבוּ — and often אַתֶּם for everyone.

Pronunciation: why spelling is hard

Most Israelis pronounce ח and כ the same, א and ע both as nothing, ט and ת the same, ק and כּ the same. When you write a word you heard, you have to remember which letter it uses. That's exactly what the writing cards train.
