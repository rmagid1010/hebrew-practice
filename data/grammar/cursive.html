Reading cursive כְּתַב יָד

Israelis type in print but write by hand in cursive script (כְּתָב רָהוּט). You'll see it on notes from neighbors, café chalkboards, whiteboards, birthday cards, your ulpan teacher's corrections — so it's worth learning to read it, even if you never write it.

Good news
  • It's the same alphabet, letter for letter — just different shapes. Spelling doesn't change.
  • Letters are not joined like English cursive. Each letter stands alone.
  • Handwriting never has niqqud — so reading cursive is also good practice for reading without vowels.

Letters that look nothing like print

Several letters are easy, because the cursive is just a rounded print shape: ב ח כ ם ס פ ר ת. Focus on the ones that change completely:

CursivePrintThink of it as…
אאa stroke + backwards c ("lc")
גגa little hook with a tail
דדa flat-topped 3
זזa small zig-zag, like 5
טטa C with a curl on top
ללa tall loop, like ℓ

Pairs that are easy to confuse

  • ד and צ — both look like a 3. ד has a flat top; צ is rounder and sits lower.
  • ו, ן and י — length is everything: י is tiny and high, ו reaches the line, ן drops below it.
  • כ and ב — ב has the extra base line.
  • ס and ם — both are loops; ם usually ends with a small flick.
  • ה and ח — ה has a separate stroke on the left; ח is one connected shape.
  • ג׳ ז׳ צ׳ — the geresh is written just like in print.
